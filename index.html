    01Overview

    Abstract

    We use recursive self-improvement (RSI) for a process in which acquired improvements strengthen a system’s capacity for further improvement: the system gets better at getting better†. Here the system includes its procedures and inherited methods; its base model may or may not stay fixed.

    We approach this through cumulative culture. Experience persists in learned practices, routines and artifacts (Henrich, 2016; Levitt and March, 1988); earlier instrument designs inform later ones (Nersessian, 2009); existing technologies and the knowledge of how to combine them support further invention (Arthur, 2007); and culturally acquired content may strengthen the very capacity to build on what is inherited (Mesoudi and Thornton, 2018). SCiEL turns this feedback between inheritance and innovation into a mechanism for search.

    Its individuals are demes, subpopulations of programs with their own lineages, and research cells, standing questions that write and test their own tools. What they learn becomes culture: hypotheses tested as experiments, claims with measured effects in a shared knowledge base, tools, and the policy and world model that steer the search. Culture passes to others only after a trial at the recipient, and it shapes how the population grows: which lineages merge, where stalled demes respawn, which seeds and portfolios start new ones. Every decision is randomized and logged with its probability, so every run is a randomized experiment on itself. We study SCiEL in the Empira arena with local inference on an open 27B model, both with the weights frozen and with LoRA adapters trained during the run.

    The research is ongoing. This page shows the mechanism on real runs and some results so far.

    † A concise formulation of recursive self-improvement: a system “gets better at getting better” (Yampolskiy, 2015).

    What this is about

    1. Getting better at getting better. An improvement is recursive when it strengthens the capacity for the next one. In SCiEL the procedures improve along with the programs, and what one run learns is exported for the next. Most systems that evolve programs keep the best programs and forget how they got there.
    2. Individuals learn, cultures accumulate. A skill outlives the person who found it because it is taught, tried by the learner and dropped when it fails. In SCiEL a hypothesis is tested as an experiment; what holds becomes a claim in the knowledge base or a tool; it is delivered to other demes and kept only if the recipient’s trial confirms it.
    3. The population is shaped by what it learned. Crossing merges lineages that each solved part of the task; stalled demes respawn where the world model sees unexplored promise; new demes start from seeds and portfolios of distinct approaches, so an idea can survive long enough to pay.
    4. A self-modification is an experiment. Every choice, which deme, which parent, which operator, when to open research, is drawn from a logged distribution. The system can therefore estimate, from its own logs and without bias, whether a change to itself helped before it keeps it.
    5. The landscape belongs to the model. How hard a task is depends on how often the model’s edit beats its parent, πε. Culture changes what the model sees, so it changes πε. That is the lever recursive improvement pulls, and it can be measured.

    03Which tasks

    Why not one model, one climber, or a swarm. A hard problem is a long climb through a landscape the model sees only one edit at a time: most edits go down, some go off the feasible set, and local bumps stop a greedy climber. A single model sampled many times never chains steps. A single climber chains them but stops on the first bump. A swarm splits the budget and its members never learn from each other. SCiEL is built for the climb: demes explore in parallel, a policy learns which way pays, a world model screens edits before they cost an evaluation, and culture moves what one deme learned to the others.

    One call samples edits of the seed independently. One climber keeps one line and accepts improvements. Swarm runs five such lines that share nothing. SCiEL runs five demes with a learned proposal policy (the halo around each head, stretched along recent progress), a world model that fits the local slope and screens proposals before they are evaluated (hollow rings), culture that moves a lagging deme to the leader after a trial (crimson arcs), and research cells that probe far when a line stalls (violet arcs). The bars below average 30 runs of each mode; switch components off to see what each one is worth. In this landscape research cells add little: the slope always leads somewhere.

    Does your task fit? Three rules, checked before any compute is spent: the check is exact or survives leak probes; the headroom above the best reference exceeds 4ε; and the upper bound of π at the frontier is at least 5/B. sciel task measures them from your materials before any search starts.

    The definition in full

    A task is ⟨X, F, h, h*, M, B⟩: candidates the model writes, a feasible set, the objective the run sees and the true one, the model's edit operator, and the budget. It is read on five axes: solution space, feasible set, noise (the radius ε below which a difference is not real), the landscape for the model (πε at the frontier) and verifiability. Because the landscape is defined by the model's operator, it moves as culture changes what the model sees.

    What fits, in plain words

    1. You can check an answer you can trust.Exactly, like arithmetic or a replay, or on data the search never saw, like held-out days or a held-out regime.
    2. The best known answer can still be beaten.There is room above it, and that room is larger than the noise in your score.
    3. Progress hides behind rare long shots.Most tries fail, and the ones that matter are bold guesses a single attempt would never risk. SCiEL makes long shots affordable: a population holds them, niches keep odd ones alive, a world model screens them before they cost an evaluation, a policy learns which kinds pay, and culture spreads the rare hit.

    Poor fits: puzzles solved in one shot, tasks already at their ceiling, and anything without a score you would bet on.

    Not sure? Point sciel task at a folder of materials. It drafts the task, audits all three rules before any compute is spent, and repairs what it can.

    04How it works

    Culture is a tree with chords.

    Findings, by how far they travelled

    Sector = deme. Distance from the centre = generation. Lines join a parent to its child. Filled dot = still in the population, hollow = dropped, bigger = higher score. The run replays on a loop; hover a program for its score and lineage.

    Does culture raise the odds? A randomized test.

    First chart: probability that a step succeeds, with and without a finding from another deme in the prompt, weighted by the inverse of the logged probability of each choice. Second chart: the difference by phase of the run with 95% intervals from a bootstrap over runs, and each run's own difference as a dot, sized by its number of steps.

    From a question to a transmitted method.

    From question to chord, this run

    Hover a stage name for what it counts. Each research cell is a standing question. A step asks the model to plan an experiment, write a tool, run it in a sandbox and conclude. A conclusion becomes a finding; a finding is shared, adopted for trials by other demes, and drawn as a chord when it reaches a child there.

    A world model of the search. Before the language model is called, SCiEL forecasts whether a proposed step will raise its lineage’s best. It does not model the task; it models the search itself, from the parent, the deme, the kind of edit, an embedding of the code and the run’s history so far. It starts from a pretrained tabular model, so it is useful after a few dozen steps, and refines itself with a residual network on the run’s own rows; rows inherited from earlier runs give it a head start. Like every learned part of SCiEL, it acts only while it earns it: at each refit it must beat the base rate and the per-cell means out of fold, or it is held back.

    The world model in the loop

    1. Describeparent, deme, edit, code embedding, history
    2. Priora pretrained tabular model, useful within dozens of steps
    3. Refinea residual network on this run’s rows, plus inherited ones
    4. Gateacts only if it beats simple estimates out of fold
    5. Usescreen proposals, weigh moves, point research where it is unsure

    Its record in this run, refit by refit, is in the ladder of controllers below.

    Where the long shots live. A guess that lands somewhere new is usually worse than the current best at first. If the population kept only the best, it would be evicted before anyone could build on it. So each deme is a map of niches, one elite per cell of behaviour, and a newcomer competes only with its own cell.

    Niches, deme by deme

    Each board is a deme's map: learned niches across, score bins back to front; a bar is the best program in its cell, taller when closer to the best known value.

    Boards: each board is one deme. Across, the run's twelve learned niches; back to front, score bins; a bar is the best program in its cell, taller when closer to the best known value. Map: the same twelve niches as points: centroids of a vector-quantised codebook over a 32-dimensional embedding of the programs' code, projected onto their two main directions. A bubble's size is how many demes hold an elite there; its dots are those demes. The codebook grows and is re-fitted as the population moves, so a new kind of program opens a new niche instead of being thrown out.     Cold, warm, hot: who steers the search, and when. A run starts knowing nothing about which moves pay, and some of what it could learn takes hundreds of steps to see. So control passes through three stages, each taking over only once it has the data to do better than the stage before.

    1. Cold start · from step oneBandits choose the operator, the parent and when to refine from recent rewards alone, with a floor so every option stays tried. Nothing to learn yet, so nothing is assumed.
    2. Warm · after a few dozen stepsThe world model joins with a pretrained prior and the run’s own rows. It forecasts which steps will pay and screens the rest, but only while it beats the simple estimates out of fold.
    3. Hot · a few hundred steps, or inheritedThe RL step policy learns from the whole population’s logged decisions. A run that inherits rows and a policy from an earlier one starts here, which is the hot start.

    Culture into weights: SCiEL with DeltaServe. Everything above leaves the model untouched; culture lives in the prompts, tools and policies around it. The deepest tier of consolidation is the model itself: episode, claim, method, procedure, and finally weights. DeltaServe [13] trains and serves LoRA adapters inside the same inference engine on one GPU, hot-swapping them by name, so a run can learn while it searches.
    
  
    Training a model on its own search usually goes wrong in two ways: it learns from its own samples and collapses onto a few programs, or it learns from noisy wins. SCiEL is built to supply better data. Every step comes with a logged probability, so the training set can be reweighted for the policy that produced it, as in off-policy RL. The world model marks which transitions were genuinely informative. Niches and demes keep many kinds of program alive over long runs, so the adapter sees many routes to the frontier instead of one. And the data is transitions from a weaker parent to a stronger child that held up under re-measurement, not the best programs themselves.
    
  
    An adapter does not replace the generator. It is one more randomized arm, adopted only through the same trial at the recipient as any other piece of culture, so the base model keeps proposing and its diversity is not traded away.
