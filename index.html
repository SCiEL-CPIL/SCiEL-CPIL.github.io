Let Them Predict Novel Facts: An Independent Arena for Self-Improving AI

Did the system get better at improving, or did it learn the test?

Empira · The RSI Research Arena

Self-improving AI systems are scored on the same problems they practised on while they revised themselves, so a higher score cannot separate a better method from a better fit. Empira is an arena that credits a revision only on problems published after it was frozen, against a replay of the previous version under the same starting model and budget. The problems come from a stream of executable tasks that the arena keeps mining from new scientific papers.

Why scores on seen problems are not enough

A self-improving system rewrites part of itself, such as its prompts, its tools, its agent code or its weights, and then reports a higher score. There are two ways that score can rise. The rewrite may have made the system better at solving problems in general. Or the rewrite may have absorbed the particular problems it was scored on, because those are the problems the system worked on while it changed. Both produce the same number on the same benchmark.

Philosophy of science met this problem long before machine learning. Lakatos called a research programme progressive while its revisions predict facts that were unknown when the revision was made, and degenerating while its revisions only accommodate facts already known. The cleanest test is temporal: judge a revision on facts that did not exist yet when it was made.

Empira applies that test to self-improving AI. A revision is credited only on problems published after the revision was frozen. Because new scientific papers keep appearing, the supply of such problems does not run out, which is what lets the arena keep testing systems that keep changing.

How the arena works

The arena has three parts: a stream of dated tasks, a registry of frozen system versions, and a scoring rule that compares each version with its predecessor on tasks that came later.

01A stream of dated tasks

Empira reads papers and problem collections and turns each problem it finds into a task package: an objective, an executable evaluator, a seed program to start from, the best published human result where one exists, and the date of the earliest source that states the problem. Before a task is admitted, the seed program must run and the evaluator must reject known wrong or trivial solutions. Tasks for which no sound evaluator could be built are withdrawn with a recorded reason rather than kept with a weak one.

At the snapshot of 12 October 2026, 146 source records had produced 1,530 task records. After merging 253 duplicates and withdrawing 175 tasks, the stream held 1,102 tasks covering 693 distinct problems in 13 domains. They range from open problems in combinatorics, such as the kissing number in 11 dimensions, to electric-vehicle routing, compiler register allocation, tokamak coil design and peptide reconstruction from mass spectra.

02Frozen versions and replay

A system enters the arena as a lineage of versions. Each time it revises itself, the new version is registered with a date and frozen together with everything it changed. From then on, nothing published after that date can have influenced it.

Every version is then run by an independent worker, not by its authors, from the same starting model weights as every other system and with the same fixed budget per run. A gain therefore cannot come from a stronger model or a larger budget. On each later task the worker runs the new version and replays the previous version on the same task. Replaying matters: comparing against an old score would mix the effect of the revision with the difference in difficulty between older and newer problems.

03What counts as improvement

Three quantities come out of each comparison. The first decides whether a revision is progress; the second measures how much of its apparent gain was fitting; the third tests the strongest claim a self-improving system can make, that it got better at improving itself.

Progress
Δu(m) = u(m, later) − u(v, later) − λ · cost(m)

The gain of revision m over the replayed predecessor v on later problems, minus a charge for what the revision cost. A revision counts only when Δu > 0. The main leaderboard ranks systems by their mean Δu, with a bootstrap interval over problems.

Accommodation gap
gap(m) = Δu(m, seen) − Δu(m, later)

The part of the gain that shows up only on problems available while the revision was made. Current practice reports this part as improvement; the arena reports it separately.

Recursion
Δ²u(m) = Δu(revisions after m) − Δu(revisions after replayed v)

A revision of the method is recursive when the revisions made after it are more progressive than the revisions made after its predecessor. This turns “the system improved its ability to improve” into a claim that can fail.

A higher score on a task is not automatically a new scientific result. The arena claims a new state of the art only on the SOTA track: tasks whose optimum is still open and for which a human result is published for exactly the arena’s instance. The system must beat that result, reproduced by the task’s own evaluator, by more than the noise floor on every confirmation sample.

What we found

    The leaderboard

    The first study fixes a cutoff date. Every system registers its versions before that date, and every task published after it is a later task for all of them. The cohort has 28 self-improving systems from seven lineages, from OpenEvolve and FunSearch descendants to the Darwin Gödel Machine and GEPA, plus five baselines that do not revise themselves at all. The baselines are the control: if best-of-N sampling from the starting model matches a self-improving system on later tasks, the revisions added nothing. first study pending

    The dataset

    Every task in the stream, as listed in the paper’s appendix. Each row records what an improvement on the task would mean (its contribution type), how its evaluator was obtained, the human reference for the arena’s instance, whether it is on the SOTA track, whether its problem is the main objective of a research paper, and when it was first published.
